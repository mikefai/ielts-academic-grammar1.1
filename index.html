01

Grammatical Range · Foundation

Stop writing in short, choppy sentences

Band 5–6 writing is built almost entirely from short sentences joined by and / but / so. Band 7+ requires "a variety of complex structures" — which really just means combining two ideas into one sentence, correctly, on purpose.

How it works

A complex sentence has one main clause and at least one subordinate clause, joined by a subordinator: although, since, because, whereas, given that, provided that, while, once, as. The subordinate clause can come first (with a comma after it) or second (usually no comma).

5.5–6The population is growing. This causes problems for the environment.

7.5–9As the population continues to grow, it places increasing strain on the environment.

5.5–6Renewable energy is expensive. Governments should still invest in it.

7.5–9Although renewable energy remains costly to implement, governments should continue investing in it for long-term sustainability.

TR

Türkçe cümle kurarken genellikle kısa cümleleri "ve / ama / bu yüzden" ile bağlarız. Ancak IELTS'te yüksek puan almak için bu cümleleri TEK bir karmaşık cümlede birleştirmeniz gerekir. "Although", "since", "given that" gibi bağlaçlarla pratik yapın.

In Turkish we usually link short sentences with "and / but / so." For a high IELTS score, combine them into ONE complex sentence instead, using connectors like although / since / given that.

TIP

Before you submit any writing task, count your sentences — if more than half are simple sentences with no subordinate clause, you're capped below Band 7 regardless of vocabulary.

Sentence frames to practice with

Although [weakness / counterpoint], [your main point].

Although the plan requires significant investment, it addresses a problem no cheaper policy has solved.

As [cause], [effect].

As demand for housing has grown, prices in city centres have risen sharply.

[Fact A], whereas [contrasting fact B].

Urban areas have seen rapid growth, whereas rural populations have declined.

02

Grammatical Range · Hypothetical Reasoning

Conditionals: all four types, on command

Task 2 essays constantly need hypothetical reasoning — "if this policy were adopted…", "had this been prevented…". Mixing conditional types confidently, including inverted and mixed forms, is a direct Band 8–9 signal.

How it works

Zero = facts (if + present, present). First = real future possibility (if + present, will). Second = hypothetical present/future (if + past, would). Third = hypothetical past (if + past perfect, would have). Mixed = a past condition with a present result, or vice versa.

5.5–6If more people use public transport, pollution reduces.

7.5–9Were more people to switch to public transport, urban pollution levels would fall significantly.

5.5–6If the factory didn't close, many people wouldn't lose their jobs.

7.5–9Had the factory not closed, many families would still be relying on that income today. mixed

TR

Türkçede şart cümleleri "-se / -sa" eki ile kurulur ve İngilizcedeki gibi dört farklı zaman kalıbına ayrılmaz. Bu yüzden "If I would have known" gibi hatalar çok yaygındır — doğrusu "If I had known" olmalıdır.

Turkish forms conditionals with a single suffix (-se/-sa) that doesn't split into four English tense patterns — that's why "If I would have known" is such a common error. It should be "If I had known."

TIP

Use at least one conditional per Task 2 essay, and vary the type — a second conditional for a hypothetical policy, a third for evaluating a past decision.

Sentence frames to practice with

Were [subject] to [verb], [result] would [verb].

Were the government to raise the minimum wage, low-income households would benefit directly.

Had [subject] [past participle], [result] would have [past participle].

Had the warning been issued earlier, the damage would have been minimised.

[Subject] will [verb] unless [condition].

Traffic congestion will worsen unless public transport is significantly improved.

"If the exam was easier, more people pass."Mismatched tense between clauses"If the exam were easier, more people would pass."

03

Grammatical Range · Academic Register

Passive voice and nominalisation

Task 1 reports and formal academic claims rely on the passive voice and on turning verbs into noun phrases ("increased" → "an increase in"). Used naturally — not everywhere — this is one of the fastest ways to sound genuinely academic.

How it works

Passive = be + past participle, and it shifts focus onto the result or the data rather than the person doing the action. Nominalisation turns a verb or adjective into a noun phrase, which is typical of formal written English.

5.5–6They collected the data from 500 students.

7.5–9Data were collected from 500 students.

5.5–6The number of visitors increased a lot in 2020.

7.5–92020 saw a substantial increase in visitor numbers.

TR

Türkçede edilgen çatı ("-il / -in" eki) vardır, ama günlük konuşmada İngilizce kadar sık kullanılmaz. Akademik İngilizcede ise edilgen yapı çok daha yaygındır — özellikle Task 1'de veri anlatırken.

Turkish does have a passive form, but it's used far less than in English. Academic English leans on the passive much more heavily — especially when describing data in Task 1.

TIP

In Task 1, default to the passive for describing what happened to the data; switch to active only when you're making your own comment or comparison.

Sentence frames to practice with

[Data / object] [was/were] [past participle] by [agent, if relevant].

The survey was conducted by an independent research body.

There was a [adjective] [noun] in [topic].

There was a marked increase in youth unemployment.

It is widely believed that [claim].

It is widely believed that remote work improves work-life balance.

04

Grammatical Range · Combining Information

Relative clauses (and reducing them)

Relative clauses let you fold extra information into a sentence instead of starting a new one — the single most useful tool for describing people, places and things concisely in Speaking Part 2.

How it works

Defining clauses (no commas) identify which one you mean: who, which, that. Non-defining clauses (with commas) add extra, non-essential information. A reduced relative clause drops "who/which + be" and uses the participle directly.

5.5–6The scientists did the research. They work at Cambridge University.

7.5–9The scientists who conducted the research work at Cambridge University.

5.5–6Global warming is a big problem. It affects everyone.

7.5–9Global warming, which affects every country on Earth, is one of the most pressing issues of our time.

TR

Türkçede sıfat-fiil (participle) yapısı isimden ÖNCE gelir ve ayrı bir bağlaç kullanılmaz: "dün gördüğüm film" = "the film that I saw yesterday". İngilizcede bağlaç (who / which / that) isimden SONRA gelir. Bu ters sıra en çok karıştırılan noktalardan biridir.

Turkish puts the modifying clause BEFORE the noun with no connector at all. English puts who/which/that AFTER the noun. This reversed order is one of the most common sources of confusion.

TIP

In Speaking Part 2, use one relative clause to add detail mid-sentence instead of stopping and starting a new one — it sounds far more fluent than a string of short sentences.

Sentence frames to practice with

[Noun], which [extra non-essential info], [rest of sentence].

My hometown, which is famous for its textile industry, has changed a great deal.

[Noun] who/that [defining detail] [rest of sentence].

People who grow up in bilingual households often find language learning easier.

This is a [noun] [reduced participle clause].

This is a policy aimed at reducing youth unemployment.

Speaking Part 2Writing Task 2Writing Task 1
MistakeWhyFix
"The man who he called me is my uncle."Redundant pronoun — "who" already is the subject"The man who called me is my uncle."
"My brother who lives in Istanbul is a doctor." (only one brother)Missing commas make it sound like you have several brothers"My brother, who lives in Istanbul, is a doctor."

05

Grammatical Range · The Highest-Leverage Fix

Articles: a / an / the / nothing

This is the single most common — and most fixable — error for Turkish speakers, because Turkish has no articles at all. Left unaddressed, it alone can cap Grammatical Range and Accuracy around Band 5.5, no matter how advanced everything else is.

How it works

Use a/an for a non-specific singular countable noun (often first mention). Use the when both writer and reader know exactly which one — because it was already mentioned, because there's only one, or with superlatives. Use no article for general plurals, uncountables, and most abstract ideas.

5.5–6Internet has changed way people communicate.

7.5–9The internet has changed the way people communicate.

5.5–6I think government should build more hospital in rural area.

7.5–9I think the government should build more hospitals in rural areas.

TR

Türkçede "a / an / the" karşılığı YOKTUR — bu, Türk öğrenciler için en büyük ve en sık tekrar eden hatadır. Yazınızı bitirdikten sonra sadece artikelleri kontrol etmek için ayrı bir okuma turu yapın.

Turkish has NO equivalent of a/an/the — this is the single biggest, most repeated error for Turkish candidates. After finishing any piece of writing, do one separate read-through checking articles only.

TIP

Do a dedicated final read-through of any piece of writing looking at ONLY articles — nothing else. It's the single highest-value 90 seconds you can spend before submitting.

Sentence frames to practice with

[No article] + [general plural / uncountable noun] + [general claim].

Renewable energy is becoming more affordable worldwide.

The + [noun] + [specific claim about it].

The policy introduced last year has already reduced emissions.

As a/an + [singular profession / role], [opinion].

As a teacher, I have seen how motivation affects results.

MistakeWhyFix
"She is engineer."Singular countable job noun needs a/an"She is an engineer."
"The most countries rely on the fossil fuels."General plural nouns take no article"Most countries rely on fossil fuels."
"I saw a documentary. A documentary was about…"Second mention of the same thing needs "the," not "a""I saw a documentary. The documentary was about…"

06

Grammatical Range · Balanced Argument

Hedging: sounding certain about being uncertain

Band 7+ Task 2 essays argue a position without overstating it. Modal verbs and adverbs of probability let you make a claim precisely instead of absolutely — examiners read this as academic maturity, not weakness.

How it works

Modals of probability: may, might, could, is likely to. Hedging adverbs: arguably, potentially, to some extent. Hedging verbs: suggests, indicates, tends to. Avoid absolutist words (always, never, everyone, definitely) unless you can genuinely justify them.

5.5–6Social media is bad for teenagers.

7.5–9Social media can arguably have a negative effect on some teenagers, particularly those who use it excessively.

5.5–6This will definitely solve the problem.

7.5–9This measure is likely to alleviate the problem, although it may not fully resolve it.

TR

Türkçede fikirlerimizi genellikle doğrudan ve kesin ifade ederiz; bu saygısızlık değil, kültürel bir tarzdır. Ancak IELTS Task 2'de "may", "could", "arguably" gibi yumuşatıcı kelimeler kullanmak zayıflık değil, akademik olgunluk göstergesidir.

Turkish speakers often state opinions directly and confidently — that's a cultural style, not disrespect. But in Task 2, softening words like may/could/arguably signal academic maturity, not weakness.

TIP

Hedge your position once per paragraph, at the point where you state your claim — not in every sentence, or it reads as indecisive rather than careful.

Sentence frames to practice with

[Claim] is likely to [effect], although [limitation].

This approach is likely to reduce costs, although it may not suit every industry.

It could be argued that [claim].

It could be argued that stricter regulation would slow innovation.

[Claim], particularly [specific context].

Remote work improves wellbeing, particularly for employees with long commutes.

Writing Task 2Speaking Part 3
MistakeWhyFix
"Everyone agrees that…"Almost never verifiably true — a Band 5–6 overgeneralisation"Many people would agree that…"
"This proves that…"Overclaims certainty from limited evidence"This suggests that…"

07

Coherence & Cohesion · Flow

Linking ideas without sounding like a list

Band 6 writing is often instantly recognisable: "Firstly… Secondly… In conclusion…" on repeat. Band 7+ needs a flexible range of cohesive devices — reference words, substitution, and variety — not a formula.

How it works

Use reference words (this, these, such, the latter) instead of repeating a noun, and vary how you connect ideas beyond a fixed list of linkers. Cohesion is scored on flexibility, and examiners are explicitly instructed to penalise mechanical overuse of the same connectors.

5.5–6Firstly, pollution increases. Secondly, health problems increase. Thirdly, the cost of healthcare increases.

7.5–9Rising pollution levels contribute directly to a range of health problems, which in turn drives up the cost of healthcare.

TR

Türkçedeki "Ayrıca", "Bunun yanı sıra", "Bu bakımdan" gibi bağlaçları birebir çevirmek ("In addition to this", "In this respect") yazınızı mekanik gösterir. Bunun yerine işaret sözcükleri (this, such, which) kullanarak fikirleri bağlayın.

Directly translating Turkish connectors like "Ayrıca" or "Bu bakımdan" as "In addition to this" / "In this respect" makes writing sound mechanical. Use reference words (this, such, which) to connect ideas instead.

TIP

Read your essay back and circle every "Firstly / Secondly / Moreover." If you have more than two or three, replace at least half with a reference word (this / such / which) or subordination instead.

Sentence frames to practice with

[Idea A]. This, in turn, [effect of idea A].

Automation reduces manufacturing costs. This, in turn, allows companies to lower prices.

[Idea A], which [consequence].

Funding was cut sharply, which forced several programmes to close.

Beyond [point already made], [further point].

Beyond the environmental benefits, this policy would also create jobs.

MistakeWhyFix

Repeating the full noun every sentenceSounds unnatural and mechanicalUse "this," "these," "such an approach" to refer back

Grammatical Range · The Band 8–9 Differentiator

Inversion, cleft sentences & participle clauses

These structures rarely appear at Band 6–7. Used correctly — and sparingly — they're one of the clearest signals to an examiner that they're reading a Band 8–9 script.

How it works

Inversion after a negative/limiting adverbial reverses subject and auxiliary: Not only does X…. Cleft sentences front the important information: What matters most is…. Participle clauses reduce a clause to save words: Having considered the evidence….

7.0Governments should not only invest in renewable energy, they should also educate the public.

8–9Not only should governments invest in renewable energy, but they should also educate the public on its benefits.

7.0The most important factor is access to education.

8–9What matters most in reducing inequality is access to quality education.

TR

Bu yapıların (inversion, cleft sentence) Türkçede birebir karşılığı yoktur, bu yüzden doğal gelmeyebilir. Bunları ezberlemek yerine, bir denemede sadece 2-3 kez, doğru yerde kullanmayı hedefleyin — aşırı kullanım yapay durur.

These structures have no direct Turkish equivalent, so they can feel unnatural at first. Don't force them everywhere — aim for 2–3 uses per essay, in the right place. Overuse sounds artificial even to examiners.

TIP

Use ONE advanced structure per essay, placed at your strongest point — usually the conclusion or your best body-paragraph argument. More than two or three starts to sound forced.

Sentence frames to practice with

Not only does/did [X], but [X] also [Y].

Not only does the policy cut emissions, but it also creates thousands of jobs.

What [X] is [Y].

What matters most in this debate is long-term sustainability.

Having [past participle] [object], [main clause].

Having weighed both sides of the argument, I believe stricter regulation is necessary.

MistakeWhyFix

Overused — examiners see these in almost every script

  • "Nowadays" / "In today's modern world" as an essay opener → start with your actual position instead.
  • "Last but not least" → use "Finally" or, better, a reference word ("This final point…").
  • "There are many pros and cons" as an opener → name the specific pros and cons instead of announcing that they exist.
  • "It is a double-edged sword" → state the specific upside and downside directly; the idiom adds no precision.
  • "In conclusion" every single time → vary with "On balance," "Taking everything into account," or "Ultimately."

Key tips: quick reference before exam day

The single most useful things from every module above, in one scannable place — read this the night before your exam.

Before you write or speak

  • Decide your position before you start — indecision creates hedging and tense errors.
  • Plan 2–3 grammar structures you want to use deliberately. Don't leave range to chance.

Writing Task 1

  • Default to passive + nominalisation for the overview and data description.
  • Use precise trend vocabulary instead of repeating "increased / decreased."
  • Check every countable/uncountable noun (data, figures, number) for its article.

Writing Task 2

  • Use at least one conditional, one relative clause, and one hedge — deliberately.
  • Save your ONE advanced structure for your strongest point, usually the conclusion.
  • Hedge your opinion once per paragraph, not every sentence.

Speaking

  • In Part 2, use relative clauses to add detail without stopping to start a new sentence.
  • In Part 3, hedge naturally ("I'd say," "arguably") — it buys thinking time AND shows range.
  • Aim for accuracy over ambition — one correct complex sentence beats three broken ones.

Universal proofreading checklist (Writing)

  • Articles: one read-through for a / an / the / nothing, and nothing else
  • Subject–verb agreement, especially after long subjects
  • Passive voice used where the action matters more than the doer
  • At least one subordinate clause per paragraph
  • No repeated sentence starters — vary beyond "Firstly / Also / In conclusion"
  • Every advanced structure used sounds natural when read aloud
